  This guide is part of the [User Guides](/user-guides/overview) collection. It covers the clause-review half of Contract Guardian. For the field-extraction half (pulling dates, caps, and terms out of every document), see the [Document Insights guide](/user-guides/document-insights).

Contract Guardian measures how far a contract is from language your legal and risk teams would accept.

You write down your standard once. Coverbase then reads each contract, finds the vendor's language for each of your standards, decides which tier of acceptability it lands in, and hands your reviewer a short list of deviations with the source text quoted and highlighted. Reviewers triage that list instead of reading agreements end to end.

<Note>
  Guardian decides **which clauses deviate and how badly**, then writes the asks up for you. It does not negotiate. You still review the marked-up file and decide what to send.
</Note>

## The two halves of contract intelligence

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Document Insights" icon="file-magnifying-glass" href="/user-guides/document-insights">
    Field-level. Pulls values out of every document (effective date, liability cap, notice period, governing law), each with a page citation. Runs automatically on upload.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Clause Review" icon="scale-balanced">
    Language-level. Compares the vendor's actual clause language against the tiers you have defined as acceptable, negotiable, and unacceptable. Runs when you start a review.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

They stack: insights populate the contract record and the AI summary, and clause review scores the paper against your playbook. Set up insights first if you only have time for one; it is faster and applies to every document. Set up clause review when you are ready to standardize legal review.

## Before you start

<Steps>
  <Step title="Confirm the modules are on">
    You need the **Contracts** module, and **Clause review** on top of it. If your contract records do not show a **Review** tab, clause review is not enabled for your organization yet, so ask your Coverbase contact.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Confirm your permissions">
    Building clause sets requires the contract-update permission. If **Configuration → Clause Sets** is not in your navigation, an admin needs to grant it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Have a few real contracts loaded">
    You will want two or three executed agreements to test your clause set against, plus, ideally, a copy of your own standard paper (your template MSA or DPA), which Guardian can turn into a clause set for you.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Step 1: Get contract documents in

Clause review runs against a **contract record** and the documents linked to it.

1. Upload the agreement from **Contracts → Upload Documents**, or from the vendor's Documents tab.
2. **Confirm the document type.** Types like Master Service Agreement, Order Form, Statement of Work, NDA, DPA, Terms of Service, SLA, Software License Agreement, Contract Amendment, and Contract Addendum are what mark a document as contract paper. Type inference is good but not infallible on unusual formats, and scope decisions downstream key off the type.
3. Link every document that makes up the agreement to the same contract record: the MSA, the order form, the DPA, the security addendum, the amendments. Guardian analyzes the set, not the file.

<Frame caption="A contract record. Extracted lifecycle, value, and term dates, plus an AI summary of parties, billing, SLAs, liability, and termination.">
  <img src="/images/user-guides/analyst-contract-overview.png" alt="Contract overview with extracted terms and AI summary" />
</Frame>

<Tip>
  Amendments and addendums matter here. Guardian detects the logical components inside your linked documents. It will tell an order form apart from the MSA it hangs off, and an AI addendum apart from the DPA, and it scopes each of your clause standards to the components where that clause belongs.
</Tip>

## Step 2: Build your clause set

A **clause set** is your playbook: a named collection of reference clauses, each with the language tiers you are willing to accept. You can have several: a standard SaaS set, a stricter set for vendors touching regulated data, a professional-services set.

Go to **Configuration → Clause Sets**. There are three ways to start, and most teams use the first two together.

### Start from the clause set library

<Note>
  Coverbase ships 12 packaged clause standards you can copy into your org in one click, pre-written with severities, guidance, and full multi-tier variant language.
</Note>

Choose **Clause Set Library**, pick a template, name your copy, and create it. Between them the templates carry 311 reference clauses, already scoped to the right contract components and already tiered:

| Template | Clauses | For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enterprise SaaS Subscription | 26 | Hosted software billed per seat or per unit |
| Professional Services & Consulting | 24 | Engagement letters, SOWs, change orders |
| Data Processing & Privacy | 31 | Any vendor processing personal data for you |
| Financial Services Third-Party Risk | 33 | Vendors to a regulated financial institution |
| Healthcare & HIPAA Business Associate | 29 | Vendors touching protected health information |
| AI & Machine Learning Vendor | 27 | Products that generate, classify or decide using a model |
| Infrastructure & Hosting | 25 | Cloud, colocation, managed hosting, CDN |
| Payments & Card Data | 28 | Processors, gateways, acquirers, payment facilitators |
| Staffing & Contingent Workforce | 22 | Agencies, MSPs, umbrella companies, contractor platforms |
| Hardware & Supply Chain | 23 | OEMs, distributors, contract manufacturers |
| Marketing & Advertising Agency | 22 | Creative, media, performance marketing, influencer platforms |
| Reseller & Channel Partner | 21 | Buying through a reseller, VAR or marketplace |

Pick by what the vendor does to your risk, not by the industry you are in. A hospital buying a CRM wants the SaaS set, unless that CRM will hold patient data.

The copy is yours once you create it. Edit the language, retune the severities, archive the clauses you do not care about. Nothing syncs back to the library, so editing cannot break anything.

  Copy the library set, run it against three contracts you already know the answers on, and then tune. Starting from working language and adjusting beats starting from a blank page, and it shows you what a well-written reference clause looks like before you write your own.

### Generate a clause set from your own paper

If you have a standard template (your MSA, your DPA, your preferred terms), Guardian can turn it into a clause set.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Upload the baseline">
    On the Clause Sets page choose **Upload Baseline Contract** and submit your template as a PDF. Processing runs in the background; you can leave the page and come back.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review what it extracted">
    Guardian pulls out clause candidates with exact source text from your template, plus a suggested name, category, severity, and applicable component types. Language it cannot quote verbatim from your document is dropped rather than paraphrased, so what you get back is your own paper.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add your fallback tiers">
    Each generated clause arrives with a single **Tier 1 "Baseline"** variant, your preferred language. That is a floor, not a playbook: it marks anything that is not your exact template as needing review. Add the tiers you would accept in negotiation before you run the set against a live contract.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Activate it">
    Generated clause sets are created **inactive**, so a half-finished playbook cannot be run against a live contract. Flip it to active once the tiers are in.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Card title="Baseline contract extraction reference" icon="wand-magic-sparkles" href="/user-guides/baseline-contract-extraction">
  What extraction proposes, why it drops language it cannot quote verbatim, and the tiering you have to finish before the set is usable.
</Card>

### Import a clause set from a spreadsheet

If your standards already live in a spreadsheet, put them in the Coverbase template and upload the file instead of retyping them. One workbook can carry several clause sets, each with full multi-tier variant language.

On the Clause Sets page open **Add Clause Set**, choose **Download Import Template**, fill in the **Clauses** sheet (one row per clause, with rows sharing a **Clause Set** value grouped into one set), then come back and choose **Import from Spreadsheet**.

Every row is reported back by its spreadsheet row number as created, already existing, or an error, and one bad row never blocks the rest. Clauses whose identifier is already in the target set are skipped rather than overwritten, so a stale re-upload cannot replace negotiated language.

<Card title="Spreadsheet import and export reference" icon="file-import" href="/user-guides/clause-set-spreadsheet-import">
  Every column explained, the variant format, what happens on re-upload, how to export a set back out, and a troubleshooting table.
</Card>

### Build one by hand

Choose **Add Clause Set**, name it, then add reference clauses one at a time. This suits a narrow, high-stakes set (an AI addendum standard, say), and rarely a full playbook.

## Step 3: Understand a reference clause

Every reference clause is one standard you hold vendors to.

| Field | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| **Clause identifier** | Your stable reference, like `SVR-001`. Use it in tickets and email so everyone means the same clause. |
| **Name** | What reviewers see. Use the heading a lawyer would recognize. |
| **Severity** | Low, Medium, High, or Critical. Drives triage order and grouping, but does *not* change the verdict. Reserve Critical for clauses that would block a signature. |
| **Rationale** | Why the clause matters. Written for humans; it is the context a reviewer needs when they hit a deviation. |
| **Guidance** | Written for the AI. This is what tells the matcher how to find and evaluate the clause. See below. |
| **Applicable component types** | Which parts of a contract this clause should be found in. Leave empty to apply everywhere. |
| **Variants** | Your acceptability tiers, covered in the next step. |

### Writing guidance the matcher can use

Guidance is the instruction the model reads when hunting for vendor language. The library clauses are worth copying as a format. This is the shape that works, from the packaged Limitation of Liability clause:

```text Guidance
Aliases:
- Limitation of Liability
- Liability Cap
- Limitations on Liability
- Maximum Liability

Typical contract types:
- MSA
- SaaS Agreement
- Services Agreement

Extraction signals:
- ALL CAPS BLOCKS containing 'IN NO EVENT'
- phrases: 'aggregate liability', 'total liability', 'fees paid'
- multipliers: '1x', '2x', 'twelve (12) months'
- carve-outs: 'except for', 'notwithstanding', 'shall not apply to'

Common deviations:
- Cap is unspecified or 'unlimited'
- Cap is asymmetric (one party capped, other uncapped)
- Carve-outs are too broad
- Missing carve-outs for IP indemnity, data breach, confidentiality
```

Four sections: **aliases** the clause hides under, **contract types** it appears in, **extraction signals** (distinctive phrases, formatting quirks, numbers), and **common deviations** you have seen. You do not need all four on every clause, but aliases and signals make the biggest difference.

## Step 4: Write your risk-tier variants

This is the step that determines whether Contract Guardian is useful or noisy.

A **variant** is one version of the clause language at one level of acceptability, on a scale of 1 to 5. Coverbase matches the vendor's actual language to the closest variant, and the tier of that variant becomes the verdict:

| Risk tier | Meaning | Resulting clause state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | Your preferred language | **Conforming** |
| **2** | Acceptable without escalation | **Conforming** |
| **3** | Negotiable; a reviewer should look | **Needs review** |
| **4** | Materially worse than standard | **Non-conforming** |
| **5** | Unacceptable | **Non-conforming** |

Two more rules apply on top of the tier mapping:

- **Low-confidence matches never auto-pass.** If the matcher is not confident enough in the match, the clause is set to **Needs review** regardless of which variant it landed on.
- **Language found outside its scope is escalated.** If a clause turns up in a component it was not scoped to, or if the language materially conflicts with the matched variant, the clause goes to **Needs review** rather than being scored.

### How many tiers do you need?

You do **not** need all five on every clause. Start with three and add as you learn:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Tier 1: your ask">
    The language you would put in your own paper. If you generated the set from a baseline contract, this is already filled in.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Tier 3: your fallback">
    What you would sign after a round of negotiation without escalating. Most real vendor paper lands on this tier, and it is what routes a clause to a person for review.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Tier 5: your walkaway">
    Language you will not accept. Writing it down turns "this feels bad" into a consistent, defensible verdict.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Add tiers 2 and 4 once you have seen enough contracts to know where the intermediate lines sit.

<Warning>
  A clause with only a Tier 1 variant flags nearly everything, because almost no vendor's paper matches your template word for word. That is the most common cause of a noisy first review, and the usual state of a freshly generated baseline clause set. Add a fallback tier before judging the false-positive rate.
</Warning>

### Label your variants like a negotiator

The label shows up next to the verdict, so make it say what the tier is. From the library set:

- Tier 1: *"Vendor-favorable: 1x fees, 12 month lookback, broad consequential carve-out"*
- Tier 3: *"Balanced: 2x fees with carve-outs for confidentiality and data breach"*
- Tier 5: *"Unacceptable: liability disclaimed in full"*

A reviewer reading *"Balanced: 2x fees with carve-outs"* knows what they are looking at. A reviewer reading *"Tier 3"* has to go find out.

## Step 5: Scope clauses to contract components

Guardian first detects the **components** inside your linked documents (the MSA, the DPA, the order form, the SLA, the NDA, the amendment, the SCCs, the AI addendum, and so on), then evaluates each reference clause only where it belongs.

Set **Applicable component types** on each reference clause accordingly. A data-processing standard belongs on the DPA and MSA. A pricing standard belongs on the order form. Governing law belongs everywhere, so leave it unscoped.

Scope decides what a *missing* clause means:

| Situation | Result |
| --- | --- |
| The clause's component is present, but no matching language found | **Missing**: a genuine gap in the vendor's paper |
| The clause's component is not in this contract at all | **Not applicable**: correctly ignored |
| A component could not be classified, so the answer is uncertain | **Needs review**: flagged for a human |

<Tip>
  Unscoped clauses (empty component types) are evaluated against everything, so a missing one always reads as **Missing**. Scope the clauses that only apply to one kind of paper, and your Missing list stays meaningful.
</Tip>

## Step 6: Run a review

A review runs in five steps, shown in the rail at the top of the page. You move through them in order, and the rail says where you are.

<Frame caption="The five steps of a review. The rail marks the steps behind you as done.">
  <img src="/images/user-guides/contracts-review-steps.png" alt="Review step rail: select documents, analyze, review results, decide, communicate" />
</Frame>

| Step | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Select documents** | You choose the standards to check against and the documents to read. |
| **2. Analyze the contract** | Coverbase reads the documents and matches every reference clause. |
| **3. Review results** | You see the counts and pick what to work. |
| **4. Decide** | You record a verdict on each flagged clause. |
| **5. Communicate** | You draft the follow-up and export the redlined Word file. |

Activate the clause set before you start. An inactive set cannot be run. Then open the contract, go to the **Review** tab, and choose **Start review**.

### Select documents

<Frame caption="Step 1. Pick the standards to check against and the documents to read.">
  <img src="/images/user-guides/contracts-review-select-documents.png" alt="Select documents step showing suggested standards, other standards, documents to analyze, reviewer, and job estimate" />
</Frame>

Four things sit on this step.

**Suggested for this contract** lists the clause sets that cover components this contract has. **Other standards** lists the rest, and tells you their clauses will mostly come back as not applicable. Pick at least one.

**Documents to analyze** lists every live document on the contract. All of them are selected. Clear the checkbox on any document you want left out, and the review skips it.

Each document shows its type, when it was uploaded, and who uploaded it, so you can tell two similar files apart before you commit to reading both.

<Note>
  Start from everything selected and take documents out, rather than adding them one at a time. A review that reads a subset can miss a clause that was in the file.
</Note>

**Reviewer** puts the review in someone's work queue. You can leave it unassigned.

**Job estimate** tells you how many clauses, standards, and documents the run covers once you have picked a standard. Only in-force language is checked, so superseded clauses are excluded.

If the contract has no documents yet, this step says so and links you to the Documents tab. The start button stays disabled until there is something to read.

### Analyze the contract

Coverbase extracts the text, detects components, and matches every reference clause across the documents you selected. If the vendor has a completed assessment, it then layers that evidence onto each flagged clause.

A progress indicator runs while it works. You can navigate away and come back.

<Note>
  Each run takes a **snapshot** of the clause set at the moment it starts. Editing a reference clause mid-run does not change the verdicts of a review already in flight, and old runs stay readable against the standard they were judged by.
</Note>

### Start another review

One review runs at a time, but a finished review does not lock the contract. **Start a new review** sits in the header on every step after the analysis. Use it when a run read the wrong documents, when you picked the wrong standard, or when new paper has landed since the last run.

Starting a new review takes you back to step 1 with the pickers open. Previous runs stay readable.

## Step 7: Work the results

The last three steps of the rail are yours: read the results, decide each clause, then send what you decided.

### Review results

<Frame caption="A clause review. Vendor language scored against your acceptable, fallback, and unacceptable tiers, by severity.">
  <img src="/images/user-guides/analyst-clause-review.png" alt="Clause review showing deltas from preferred language" />
</Frame>

The results list opens on **Flags**: the clauses that came back Non-conforming or Needs review and have not been triaged yet. Switch to **All** to see everything, including what conformed.

#### What each row tells you

| Element | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **State** | Conforming, Needs review, Non-conforming, Missing, or Not applicable |
| **Matched variant** | Which of your tiers the vendor's language landed on |
| **Confidence** | How sure the matcher was. Low confidence forces Needs review |
| **Source** | The document and page the language came from |
| **AI analysis** | A few bullets on why it landed where it did |
| **Assessment insights** | A suggested disposition drawn from the vendor's latest completed assessment |

Group by **status**, **severity**, or **confidence**, sort by severity, and search by clause name. On a 40-clause set against a heavily negotiated MSA, grouping by severity and working top-down is the fastest path.

#### Assessment insights

When the vendor has a completed assessment, Guardian cross-references the clause against that evidence and suggests a disposition: **No action**, **Revise language**, **Add clause**, or **Escalate**, with citations back into the assessment.

A weak audit-rights clause matters more when the vendor's assessment already shows control gaps, and less when they hold a clean SOC 2. The suggestion is input for your judgment rather than a verdict, and it sits separately from the clause state.

#### Triage every flag

Triaging is the review. Marking a clause removes it from the flagged list while leaving the system's verdict visible, so the record shows both what the AI concluded and what you decided.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="No action" icon="check">
    Reviewed and fine as written. The most common outcome on Needs-review clauses once a human reads them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Accepted risk" icon="shield-halved">
    Materially worse than your standard, and you are signing anyway. This is your audit trail for a conscious exception.
  </Card>
  <Card title="False positive" icon="circle-xmark">
    The matcher got it wrong. Feed these back into the reference clause's guidance so the next review does better.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Create finding" icon="flag">
    Escalate to a tracked finding with an owner and a due date. Triage flips to **Action created** automatically, and the finding stays linked to the clauses that produced it.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

Select several clauses at once to triage or create a single finding covering all of them, which suits a case where one negotiation thread touches liability, indemnity, and insurance together.

### Decide

The **Decide** step gives you one clause at a time in a three-pane console.

<Frame caption="The decide console. Playbook positions on the left, the redline and your decision in the middle, the source document and clause facts on the right.">
  <img src="/images/user-guides/contracts-review-decide.png" alt="Three-pane decide console showing playbook positions, suggested redline, decision options, and the source document" />
</Frame>

| Pane | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| **Left** | Your playbook positions for this clause, with the guidance you wrote. Select a position to redraft the redline against it. |
| **Middle** | The suggested redline, with your decision underneath. |
| **Right** | The source document, and the clause card with state, severity, confidence, and the AI analysis. |

Drag the dividers to resize the panes. Your widths are remembered for the next clause and the next review.

Four decisions sit under the redline. Press **1** to **4** to pick one, or click it.

| Key | Decision | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | Accept as-is | Closes the clause. |
| **2** | Flag for the vendor | Adds it to the follow-up you send at the end. |
| **3** | Accept the risk | Records the exception with an owner and an expiry date. |
| **4** | Not applicable | Marks the standard as not relevant to this contract. |

Press **Enter** to record the decision and move to the next clause. **Previous clause** and **Next clause** sit in the header with your position in the queue between them.

**Show every clause** switches the queue between the clauses that need you and all of them, including what conformed.

### Communicate

<Frame caption="The Communicate step. A follow-up draft built from the flagged clauses, with the Word export beside it.">
  <img src="/images/user-guides/contracts-review-communicate.png" alt="Communicate step showing the generated follow-up draft and the redlined Word file button" />
</Frame>

The follow-up draft is built from the clauses you flagged, quoting your standard and the vendor's language. Nothing sends. Copy it into your own email or escalate it to a finding.

#### Download the redlined Word file

If the review read a Word document, choose **Download redlined Word file** next to the draft. You get the contract's own `.docx` back with:

- The contract's wording struck through and your playbook's wording inserted, as tracked changes
- One comment per clause carrying the clause name, the reasoning, and the language you are asking for

Open it in Word and the changes appear in the Review ribbon your vendor already uses. Send it as an attachment, or accept and reject changes yourself first.

<Note>
  Every clause goes into the file except the ones you marked **Not applicable**. Accepting the risk still sends the ask: accepting a deviation internally is not the same as withdrawing it from the vendor.
</Note>

A clause whose wording already matches your standard produces no tracked change. A clause the export cannot locate in the document is named in a comment rather than dropped silently.

<Note>
  The export needs a Word document. A contract that only ever arrived as a PDF has nothing to mark up, and the button tells you so when you click it.
</Note>

#### When nothing was flagged

<Frame caption="A run that flagged nothing offers a new review instead of an empty draft.">
  <img src="/images/user-guides/contracts-review-nothing-flagged.png" alt="Communicate step with no flagged clauses, offering to start a new review" />
</Frame>

If no clause was flagged, there is nothing to draft. The step says so and offers **Start a new review** instead. That is usually the right move when a run read the wrong documents.

## Step 8: Iterate

Your first review is a draft of your playbook, not a verdict on the vendor.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Too many flags" icon="triangle-exclamation" defaultOpen>
    Almost always missing middle tiers. If your clauses only have Tier 1 and Tier 5, everything realistic lands in between and gets escalated. Add Tier 3 fallback language for the clauses generating the most noise.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Clauses come back Missing that are plainly in the contract" icon="magnifying-glass">
    Either the component scope is wrong (the clause is scoped to DPA but the language lives in the MSA), or the guidance does not name the alias the vendor used. Fix the guidance first; it is the cheaper change.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Everything lands on Needs review with low confidence" icon="gauge-low">
    Usually a text-quality problem. Scanned or image-only PDFs give the matcher little to work with. Re-upload text-based versions where you can.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Rerun after you change something" icon="arrows-rotate">
    Reviews do not re-run themselves when you edit a clause set or add a document. Use **Start a new review** from the flow header, or **Rerun** from the run actions menu on the Review tab. Either one snapshots your current clause set, and starting a new review lets you change the documents as well as the standard.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Reference clauses are versioned" icon="clock-rotate-left">
    Editing a reference clause creates a new version and marks it current. Older reviews still show the version they ran against, and you can open a clause's version history at any time. Edit freely; the record is kept.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

<Tip>
  Track your false-positive triages for the first month. Each one points at a specific reference clause whose guidance or variants need work, and fixing them is what makes a clause set reliable.
</Tip>

## A workable rollout

<Steps>
  <Step title="Week 1: copy and calibrate">
    Copy the **SaaS Vendor Risk Clause Set** from the library. Run it against three executed contracts you already know well. Compare Guardian's verdicts to your own and note every disagreement.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Week 2: tune the noisy clauses">
    Fix the five clauses that produced the most disagreement: add fallback tiers, sharpen guidance, correct component scope. Ignore the clauses that already work.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Week 3: add your own paper">
    Upload your template MSA as a baseline contract to generate a second clause set reflecting your own standard, then merge the best of both into the set you will run day to day.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Week 4: put it in the workflow">
    Start assigning reviewers on new contracts so reviews land in work queues, and make triage the definition of done for legal review.
  </Step>
</Steps>
