Contract Guardian extracts clauses, detects missing clauses, analyzes liability and indemnity, surfaces AI-usage provisions, and flags deviations from an organization-defined playbook. Rerun it against amendments and addenda so the contractual control surface stays aligned with the live risk profile maintained in Supplier Radar.

The core idea: you write down your standard once, as tiers of acceptable language. Coverbase then finds the vendor's version of each clause, decides which tier it lands in, and gives your reviewer a short list of deviations with the source text quoted and highlighted, instead of an agreement to read end to end.

<Note>
  Guardian decides which clauses deviate and how far, then marks the asks up for you. It does not negotiate. A person reviews the marked-up file and decides what to send.
</Note>

## Clause sets and risk tiers

A **clause set** is your playbook. Each **reference clause** in it is one standard you hold vendors to, carrying a severity, guidance that tells the matcher how to find the clause, the contract components it applies to, and a set of **variants**: versions of the clause language at different levels of acceptability, tiered 1 to 5.

Coverbase matches the vendor's actual language to the closest variant, and the tier drives the verdict:

| Risk tier | Meaning | Clause state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1-2 | Preferred or acceptable | **Conforming** |
| 3 | Negotiable, warrants a look | **Needs review** |
| 4-5 | Materially worse, or unacceptable | **Non-conforming** |

Two guardrails sit on top: a low-confidence match is escalated to **Needs review** rather than auto-passing, and language found outside the components a clause was scoped to is escalated the same way. Where no language is found, the result is **Missing** when the relevant component is present in the contract, and **Not applicable** when it isn't.

## Draft versioning and redline history

A contract is a family of documents, not a file, and Guardian is built on that model. Drafts, redlines, executed originals, amendments, restatements and exhibits are all held together and ordered, so "what did we agree, and how did we get here" is answerable without opening six PDFs.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Typed lineage between documents" icon="diagram-project">
    Coverbase draws a typed relationship between any two documents in a family: **amends**, **restates**, **attaches to**, **redline of**, and **duplicate of**, each recorded with the evidence that produced it. A version chain is data, not a naming convention.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Redlines are their own component" icon="pen-line">
    A marked-up draft is detected from its tracked changes and its content, and routed to the **redline and negotiation history** component. It is kept for the record, and it is **never in force**: an unsigned Net 45 draft can never compete with the signed Net 60 amendment for the payment-terms slot.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Supersession is explicit" icon="layer-group">
    Amendments resolve as an ordered series where each supersedes the last, with the effective date deciding. A superseded instance stays visible in the history rather than being deleted, and anything contributing nothing in force, a redline or a duplicate, renders struck through.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Every answer cites its source" icon="quote-left">
    Each resolved term names the document, the page and the effective date it came from: "Net 60, from Amendment 3 effective 1 Jan 2024, supersedes Net 30 in the master agreement." You can open the source span from the answer.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

Two further version-history properties matter for legal review:

- **Conformed copies are handled deliberately.** A master re-issued with its amendments already incorporated is recognized as such, and you choose whether the absorbed amendments stay as separate terms or are marked incorporated. Counting the same amendment twice is the failure mode this exists to prevent.
- **Clause sets are versioned.** Editing a reference clause never rewrites the history of reviews already run against the previous wording, so a review from last quarter still shows the standard it was actually judged against.

### The review's own history

Alongside the document lineage, each Guardian run keeps its own record: the system verdict per clause, the matched variant and tier, the confidence, the evidence with page citations, and the human triage decision layered on top. Re-run Guardian after an amendment and you get the new verdict beside the old one, so what changed in the controlled obligation surface is visible rather than inferred.

Where the contract arrived as a Word file, Guardian can return it with the counterparty's wording struck and your playbook's inserted as tracked changes, plus a comment per clause carrying the ask. It opens in the Review ribbon your counterparty already uses, which keeps the negotiation in the format legal teams actually redline in.
